Avoiding Scams

By Bao Duy Nguyen

In cybersecurity, scams are very common. However, scammers can steal your personal information, such as your email address, password, date of birth, your Social Security Number, and even your credit/debit card number. It is important that you protect your devices from security breaches. In this article, we’ll break it down on what the scam is, what the examples are, and some tips to help protect your computer from scammers stealing your information.

What is a scam?


A scam is a common trick as an attempt to scare or defraud people after believing their trust that there is “a problem with their computer.” According to the Federal Trade Commission, scammers (a group of computer hackers who pretend to be a representative from their respective companies) can tell lies to get your financial information or remote access to your computer. In short, they want to steal your money, or even worse, your identity.


A recent report from the FTC showed (at the time of this article) that there are over 1,372,927 fraud reports, around 562,359 are people that have reported fraud with their money loss. In total, over $6993 million ($6,993,000,000) have been reported as loss. However, a 2025 report showed that 3,045,796 people have reported a scam, with 1,117,253 people reporting a scam also having their money stolen. In total, over $16,058 million ($16,058,000,000) have been reported as loss. This shows that the number of fraud reports is growing at a rapid speed.


What does a scam look like?


As mentioned above, a scammer can pretend to be a representative of their respective company. One of the most common types of scams is technical support, but the FTC shows that an imposter scam (a type of scam when a scammer lies and pretends to be someone else “they’re not to trick you into giving them money or personal information”) is the number one top fraud list (FTC). A scammer then lies to a user that there is a problem with their account and they demand that they call or text a message to send them their personal information (such as their email address, password, social security number, and even their credit card number). In most cases, scammers would also demand that a user should go to their store and buy a gift card to pay with them. Retail stores (either physical or online) do not ask for the gift card number.

Remember, do not call or text a phone number, or reply to the message that you see in this section; these are meant to be examples only. Respective companies do not ask you to enter your personal information or your money. Please note that some websites have been blurred out for security reasons. Ways to protect yourself from being scammed


There are numerous ways to protect yourself from being scammed. We have a couple of tips that we think would benefit you.

  1. Check the sender’s email address before sending or complying
    • The sender’s email address should be the official email address that you have received (e.g., @outlook.com, @gmail.com, @uw.edu, @computingforall.org), as well as the official name of the sender. If this is not the official sender’s email address (e.g., a sender that pretends to be Microsoft - @microsoft.com, but turns out to be something else), then it is a scam. It is always a good idea to check with your sender directly to resolve some issues you’re having.
  2. Always check for spelling, grammar, and punctuation errors by reading through it.
    • A scammer pretends to let the user know that there’s a problem with their credit count account. However, there may be some spelling, grammar, and/or punctuation errors that you’ll end up realizing that it is a scam. Carefully check for these errors and verify that it comes from the official sender's email address.
  3. Do not click on suspicious links!
    • Clicking on suspicious links can expose you to various security risks. If they demand that you resolve your locked account or you pay the scammer some money through a link, DON'T DO IT! Instead, pause and reflect before you act by confirming that it's from a legitimate source.
  4. Never pay a scammer with a credit/debit card.
    • If you have accidentally paid a scammer with your credit card, contact your authorized bank and ask them to reverse the transaction to give your money back. Additionally, retail stores do not ask you to pay a scammer with a gift card. If that happens, contact your gift card company immediately and ask for your money back.
  5. Never pay a scammer with a credit/debit card.
    • If you have accidentally paid a scammer with your credit card, contact your authorized bank and ask them to reverse the transaction to give your money back. Additionally, retail stores do not ask you to pay a scammer with a gift card. If that happens, contact your gift card company immediately and ask for your money back.
  6. Report it!
    • Report any scams at either https://fightcybercrime.org/ or https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/. Your report does make a difference!

AI-powered scams - BEWARE!


With artificial intellegence (AI) rising in recent years, so are the AI-powered scams. These can include, but are not limited to, phone calls that claim to be from someone that you thought you would know (but in reality, it's from a scammer itself), deepfake videos inserted into social media and/or video game apps, and imposter websites, as well as text messages. They make it look like they're legitimate, but it turns out, scammers are using free and/or low-cost AI systems to demand that you steal your personal information, and they may even get your computer infected with security risks.

In short, if scammers (using artificial intellegence to claim that they're somebody that you thought you know and trust) want your bank information and/or other sensitive information while on your phone, HANG UP IMMEDIATELY AND REPORT IT TO THE FTC! A good rule of thumb is to ask for the safe word (e.g., rubber ducky, Fred Flintstone, etc.); if the caller doesn't know, then you'll know it's a scam, and you'll want to hang up immediately.

Additionally, if you are encountering an ad that promotes a useless claim made up of AI while looking at social media, think before you act, and report AI-powered scam ads through your social media, or at https://www.ic3.gov/.
